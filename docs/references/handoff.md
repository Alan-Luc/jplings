# Project Handoff: Personalized Japanese Learning System

Working concept: Rustlings for Japanese
Stage: Discovery complete enough to begin product and technical planning
Timeline: Approximately six months until Japan trip
Date: October 2026

## 1. Mission

Design a personalized Japanese learning environment inspired by Rustlings.

The objective is not to create a general-purpose language-learning platform. It is to build an effective, enjoyable learning system tailored to one learner with fragmented but sometimes surprisingly sophisticated prior Japanese knowledge.

Core hypothesis:

Structured learning can accelerate progress by connecting existing fragments of implicit linguistic knowledge, correcting misconceptions, filling foundational gaps, and converting receptive understanding into spontaneous conversational ability.

The system should combine:

- Guided learning material.
- Hands-on exercises requiring active participation.
- Quizzes and mastery checks.
- Listening comprehension.
- Spoken exercises using STT/TTS.
- Dynamic conversational practice.
- Cumulative review and retention testing.

Preserve Rustlings' simplicity, progressive structure, immediate feedback, and emphasis on learning by doing.

Avoid unnecessary engineering complexity.

## 2. Learner goals

Primary objective

Develop independent, everyday conversational Japanese before a trip to Japan in approximately six months.

The aspirational benchmark is the communicative independence of a Japanese first-grade student, not literal native-speaker proficiency.

The learner wants to:

- Understand and participate in spontaneous everyday conversations.
- Construct original sentences rather than rely on memorized phrases.
- Describe experiences, plans, preferences, feelings, and opinions.
- Ask questions and understand responses.
- Sustain conversations on familiar topics.
- Navigate restaurants, shopping, transportation, and everyday social situations.
- Ask for clarification and recover from misunderstandings.
- Read common signs, menus, and accessible Japanese material.

Speaking and listening are the highest priorities.

Reading is important, particularly for manga and real-world comprehension.

Handwriting is not a goal. Do not dedicate curriculum time to handwriting or stroke-order memorization unless explicitly requested.

Available study time

- Weekdays: 30–60 minutes daily.
- Weekends: 1–2 hours daily.
- Approximately 5–9 hours of structured study weekly.
- Additional recreational exposure through anime, manga, Japanese YouTube, and J-pop.

Sessions should accommodate varying durations without breaking curriculum progression.

## 5. Assessment findings

Seven informal assessment rounds were conducted. These are diagnostic observations, not standardized proficiency measurements.

### Round 1 — Explicit grammatical knowledge

The learner:

- Associated は with English "is."
- Recognized が好き and を信じて without understanding the particle rules.
- Recognized several verb forms and their meanings.
- Mistook the て-form for a future-related form.
- Recognized sentence-final particles as affecting conversational tone.
- Struggled with kanji-heavy written sentences.

Finding: Explicit grammar knowledge is significantly less developed than familiarity with Japanese constructions.

### Round 2 — Sentence comprehension

The learner correctly interpreted all three simple sentences presented in romaji:

1. Going to eat ramen with friends tomorrow.
2. Yesterday was hot, but today is cold.
3. I like cats, but dogs are also cute.

The learner also noticed ambiguity around the use of と.

Finding: Can understand combinations of basic Japanese vocabulary and grammatical structures, not merely isolated memorized phrases.

This was text-based romaji comprehension, not an audio test.

### Round 3 — Spontaneous production

The learner deliberately deviated from suggested English translations to express original thoughts.

Examples:

- "kinou tomodachi to ramen wo tabete"
- "neko wa ichiban doubutsu dakedo inu daikirai"
- "Nippon no meshi ga suki, dakara, nihon wo ikitai"
- "doko kimashitaka"
- "nihongo wa sugoku muzukashi"

The learner clarified that the intended meanings included "cats are the best animal, but I hate dogs" and "where did you come from?"

Finding: Can independently assemble meaningful sentences and use familiar constructions such as だけど, だから, and たい.

However, particle selection, conjugation, and sentence structure are inconsistent.

Important: Assess intended meaning separately from grammatical correctness. Do not assume the learner is translating an English prompt literally.

### Round 4 — Conjugation generalization

Attempts using 飲む:

- "nometa" instead of 飲んだ.
- "nometai" instead of 飲みたい.
- "nomenai" instead of 飲まない.
- "nomekudasai" instead of 飲んでください.
- "atsuikatta" instead of 暑かった.

The learner explicitly noted that 飲む seemed tricky and probably did not follow the same pattern as 食べる.

Finding: Understands several grammatical functions but does not yet reliably apply verb-class-specific transformations.

Crucially, the learner sometimes recognizes that their available rule may be insufficient.

Also, 飲めない is a valid form meaning "cannot drink," illustrating why evaluation should consider unintended but legitimate Japanese forms.

### Round 5 — Naturalness recognition

Correct: 4/6.

Correctly recognized:

- 猫が好き
- ラーメンを食べたい
- 今日は寒いね
- 分からない

Incorrectly selected:

- 日本を行った instead of 日本に行った.
- まだ食べた instead of もう食べた.

Finding: Recognition of familiar expressions is stronger than reliable grammatical generation.

Confidence is sometimes well calibrated, but high confidence does not guarantee correctness.

### Round 6 — Conversational interpretation

Correct: 3/5.

Correctly understood:

- もう食べました
- 大丈夫？
- まだ食べてない

Missed:

- ちょっと難しいかな… as a possible indirect refusal.
- 行こうか as "Shall we go?"

The learner later reported that 行こうか sounded familiar when mentally replayed.

Finding: Familiar conversational expressions are understood, but indirect pragmatic meaning and unfamiliar constructions remain challenging.

Acoustic familiarity may sometimes exceed recognition from written presentation.

### Round 7 — Immediate learning and transfer

After a short lesson on volitional forms:

- "yomitai" for 読もう — incorrect, substituted a familiar desire construction.
- "neyou" for 寝よう — correct.
- "hanasou" for 話そうか — correct verb form, omitted question particle.
- "Let's go home" for 帰ろうか — acceptable contextual interpretation.

The learner later clarified that 話そう was already familiar from anime.

Finding: Some immediate rule application was demonstrated, but the assessment cannot isolate new learning from pre-existing exposure.

Learning speed and long-term retention remain unmeasured.

## 7. Product philosophy

Preserve the Rustlings learning model

The core learning loop should be:

Learn → Exercise → Receive feedback → Demonstrate understanding → Progress → Revisit later.

Learning material should explain a concept clearly.

Exercises should require active participation rather than passive recognition.

Quizzes should establish whether the learner can use the concept without being guided through every step.

Prefer constructing sentences, interpreting meaning, completing transformations, and speaking over multiple-choice exercises.

Personalization should be evidence-based

Do not assume the learner has mastered a topic because an expression sounds familiar.

Track distinct dimensions:

- Recognition
- Comprehension
- Explicit understanding
- Production
- Generalization
- Confidence
- Retention

The learner should be able to move quickly through familiar material but must demonstrate transferable understanding before skipping important foundations.

Leverage existing knowledge

Use familiar expressions as entry points into new grammar.

When relevant, explain connections to:

- Previously encountered Japanese phrases.
- Existing grammatical intuitions.
- Kanji components.
- Cantonese vocabulary and shared Chinese-character roots.
- Common expressions encountered in anime or manga.

Do not force etymological or Cantonese explanations into every lesson. Use them where they genuinely improve understanding.

Prioritize spoken language

STT/TTS should be integrated into the core curriculum, not merely offered as an optional feature.

Use audio for:

- Listening comprehension.
- Spoken responses.
- Interactive conversational scenarios.
- Question-and-answer exercises.
- Listening without written prompts.
- Real-time retrieval and production.

Speech recognition success must not be treated as definitive evidence of correct pronunciation.

Dynamic conversations

Conversational exercises should be constrained by the learner's current knowledge while allowing natural variation.

Example progression:

1. Learn a grammatical construction.
2. Practice it in written exercises.
3. Recognize it in audio.
4. Produce it verbally.
5. Use it in a dynamic conversation.
6. Demonstrate retention later in a different context.

Conversations should introduce manageable novelty rather than simply repeat textbook examples.

Complement structured study with real Japanese media

The learner already consumes or intends to consume:

Manga: Self-paced reading, sentence-structure reinforcement, conversational nuance, kanji recognition. Particularly suitable for quiet reading before bed.

Anime: Familiar dialogue, spoken expressions, listening comprehension, and contextual reinforcement.

Japanese YouTube: Everyday spoken Japanese and more natural conversational variation.

J-pop: Already part of the learner's listening habits. Additional exposure, particularly to slower songs with clear vocals, may reinforce vocabulary and familiar expressions through repetition and melody.

Interactive exercises: Deliberate practice and feedback.

Dynamic conversation: Active production and real-time comprehension.

The tool should complement these activities, not attempt to replace all of them.

Potential future functionality: importing expressions encountered in manga, anime, or song lyrics and generating targeted exercises.

Music-specific functionality is not required for the MVP. Song lyrics may use poetic grammar and altered pronunciation, so music should supplement rather than replace natural conversational listening.

## 8. Product and engineering considerations

Recommended design directions

- Separate curriculum content from exercise execution.
- Represent lessons and exercises declaratively.
- Support multiple exercise and validation types.
- Use deterministic validation for constrained grammar tasks.
- Use carefully bounded LLM evaluation for open-ended responses.
- Evaluate meaning, grammatical accuracy, naturalness, and register separately.
- Accept multiple valid answers where appropriate.
- Provide specific explanations rather than generic correct/incorrect feedback.
- Track errors and revisit weak concepts.
- Include spaced retrieval and delayed retention checks.
- Track the provenance of knowledge: previously known, inferred, guessed, or explicitly learned.
- Support lightweight progress tracking and learner-profile updates.

Avoid premature complexity

Do not start by building a general-purpose learning platform.

Avoid unnecessary gamification, elaborate analytics, large adaptive-learning systems, or complex orchestration.

Prefer a small, reliable core learning loop that can be improved through actual usage.

A CLI or similarly lightweight interface is consistent with the Rustlings inspiration, but the final interface and technology choices remain open.

Curriculum quality

Use a coherent, pedagogically grounded beginner Japanese progression.

Do not rely exclusively on an LLM to invent the curriculum or determine grammatical correctness.

The curriculum must account for prerequisites and progressively introduce vocabulary, grammar, listening, and conversational skills.

The learner's existing knowledge should influence pacing and explanation, not replace a coherent foundation.

## 8A. Local Speech Infrastructure — Research Findings (October 2026)

Objective

Provide local, self-hosted speech-to-text (STT) and text-to-speech (TTS) capabilities for the Japanese learning system.

Priorities:

- No paid speech APIs, subscriptions, or usage-based fees.
- No API keys required for local inference.
- Fully local operation after downloading dependencies and model weights.
- Low latency for interactive spoken exercises.
- Efficient resource utilization.
- Containerized deployment for reproducibility and isolation.
- Minimal infrastructure and maintenance complexity.

The speech infrastructure should support the learning experience without becoming a substantial engineering project of its own.

Available hardware

The intended host machine has:

| Component | Specification |
|---|---|
| Operating system | NixOS |
| CPU | AMD Ryzen 5 5600, 6 cores / 12 threads |
| System RAM | 16 GB |
| GPU | AMD Radeon RX 9060 XT |
| GPU VRAM | 16 GB |
| Deployment | Docker Compose |

The machine has substantial GPU capacity relative to the requirements of the proposed speech models, but comparatively limited system RAM.

Prefer GPU inference where it provides meaningful benefits, without introducing unnecessary complexity.

Speech-to-text: Whisper

Proposed model: OpenAI Whisper

Preferred implementation: "whisper.cpp"

Preferred inference backend: Vulkan GPU acceleration

Whisper is an open-source, multilingual speech-recognition model supporting Japanese.

It can run entirely locally without an OpenAI API key, subscription, or per-request charges.

Why whisper.cpp?

- Supports local inference.
- Supports AMD GPUs through Vulkan.
- Provides a server implementation.
- Can be deployed in Docker.
- Avoids relying on NVIDIA CUDA.
- Offers several model sizes and quantization options.

The initial recommendation is to evaluate Whisper Small and Medium.

Start with Small and move to Medium if Japanese transcription accuracy is insufficient.

Deployment approach

Run Whisper in its own Docker container, exposing an internal HTTP endpoint.

Use Vulkan acceleration with access to the host's GPU render devices.

The official whisper.cpp project provides Vulkan-enabled container builds.

Persist model weights through a bind mount or Docker volume.

Important evaluation limitation

Successful transcription is not equivalent to correct pronunciation.

Whisper may correctly infer an intended sentence despite pronunciation mistakes.

Therefore:

- Use Whisper primarily to interpret spoken responses.
- Do not use transcription success alone as a pronunciation score.
- Evaluate semantic and grammatical correctness separately.
- Defer dedicated pronunciation assessment until a reliable methodology is established.

Remaining validation

- Verify Vulkan acceleration on the RX 9060 XT under NixOS.
- Benchmark Small versus Medium on unfamiliar Japanese utterances.
- Measure latency for short recordings.
- Evaluate transcription accuracy for beginner speech, hesitation, and grammatical errors.
- Test behavior when speech is incomplete or ambiguous.
- Determine whether streaming or incremental transcription is necessary.

Text-to-speech: Kokoro

Proposed model: Kokoro 82M

Preferred implementation: Kokoro ONNX

Initial inference backend: CPU via ONNX Runtime

Kokoro is a compact, open-weight text-to-speech model supporting Japanese.

It can run locally without API keys, subscriptions, or usage-based fees.

Japanese language support and pronunciation quality must be evaluated before committing to it as the primary instructional voice.

Why Kokoro?

- Small model relative to larger neural speech systems.
- Suitable for local inference.
- Relatively modest CPU and memory requirements.
- Can generate speech faster than real time on modern CPUs.
- Compatible with ONNX-based inference.
- Straightforward to wrap in a local HTTP service.

CPU versus GPU decision

GPU acceleration was initially considered because the host has 16 GB VRAM but only 16 GB system RAM.

However, Kokoro is sufficiently small that CPU inference is the preferred starting point.

This avoids introducing additional AMD ROCm or MIGraphX dependencies solely for TTS.

The GPU can remain available for Whisper inference.

Current recommendation: Run Kokoro on CPU unless benchmarks demonstrate an actual need for GPU acceleration.

This is a provisional engineering decision, not a confirmed performance result from the target machine.

Kokoro CPU performance research

A previously discussed benchmark using Ryzen 5 5600 CPU resources reported approximately the following results for generating 33 seconds of speech:

| Implementation | Generation time | Memory |
|---|---|---|
| PyTorch CPU | 7.78 seconds | ~1.6 GB |
| ONNX FP32 CPU | 6.13 seconds | ~630 MB |
| ONNX INT8 CPU | 5.74 seconds | ~392 MB |

These figures are external benchmark observations, not measurements from the intended deployment.

They suggest Kokoro can generate speech several times faster than real time on comparable CPU hardware.

Actual results depend on model implementation, thread configuration, text length, language, and quantization.

Do not assume INT8 will always outperform FP32.

Proposed CPU configuration

Start with:

- ONNX Runtime.
- Two CPU inference threads.
- Persistent model loading.
- FP32 and INT8 benchmark comparison.
- A dedicated containerized TTS service.

Two threads are a starting point rather than a hard requirement.

Benchmark two, four, and six threads to determine the best balance between latency and desktop responsiveness.

The application should remain usable while TTS generation is running.

Kokoro performance optimization

Prioritize optimizations in approximately this order:

1. Keep the model loaded

Load the model once when the service starts.

Avoid repeated initialization for individual requests.

2. Use ONNX Runtime

Prefer the ONNX implementation over a heavier PyTorch runtime for initial CPU deployment.

3. Compare quantization variants

Benchmark FP32 and INT8 using representative Japanese sentences.

Select based on actual latency, memory consumption, and speech quality.

4. Tune CPU threading

Test different thread counts.

Avoid unnecessarily saturating the CPU for short speech requests.

5. Cache generated speech

Many structured lessons will reuse the same phrases.

Cache audio using a key derived from the text, voice, model version, and generation settings.

Repeated content should not require new inference.

6. Pre-generate predictable lesson audio

Generate upcoming exercise audio before it is needed.

For example, while the learner answers the current exercise, prepare audio for the next one.

7. Optimize time to first audio

Measure perceived responsiveness, not merely total generation throughput.

Consider chunked generation or streaming if conversational latency becomes noticeable.

Do not introduce streaming complexity until benchmarking establishes its value.

Containerized architecture

Use Docker Compose to run independent speech services.

Japanese Learning Application → Application Backend → Whisper STT Container (AMD GPU, Vulkan) and Kokoro TTS Container (Ryzen CPU, ONNX Runtime).

Whisper container

- "whisper.cpp"
- Vulkan-enabled build
- AMD GPU access through "/dev/dri"
- Internal HTTP endpoint
- Persistent model storage

Kokoro container

- Kokoro ONNX
- CPU inference
- Configurable ONNX Runtime threading
- Internal HTTP endpoint
- Persistent model storage and optional audio cache

NixOS considerations

The host should provide:

- Docker and Docker Compose support.
- Working AMD graphics drivers.
- Vulkan support.
- Access to appropriate GPU render devices.

Example NixOS configuration:

```
{
  virtualisation.docker.enable = true;
  hardware.graphics.enable = true;
}
```

This is a minimal configuration illustration, not a complete deployment specification.

GPU permissions, Vulkan drivers inside the container, model availability, and runtime compatibility still require verification.

Avoid exposing the speech service endpoints publicly unless there is a specific requirement.

Service boundaries

Keep STT and TTS independent of curriculum logic.

The learning application should interact with them through small, stable interfaces.

Conceptual operations:

transcribe(audio, language) -> transcription

synthesize(text, language, voice) -> audio

The application should not depend directly on Whisper- or Kokoro-specific internals.

This allows future replacement of either model without restructuring the lesson engine.

An OpenAI-compatible API shape may be convenient, but compatibility is optional. Do not add an unnecessary proxy or abstraction layer solely to achieve it.

Expected resource profile

The target machine should be capable of running both services simultaneously.

Expected behavior:

- Whisper primarily consumes GPU compute and VRAM.
- Kokoro consumes CPU resources during synthesis.
- Kokoro should have little CPU activity while idle.
- Both services retain some system RAM for model weights and runtime overhead.
- Docker itself introduces relatively little overhead compared with model inference.

GPU acceleration does not eliminate system RAM usage.

Actual combined RAM consumption and latency must be measured on the target machine.

Speech-specific acceptance criteria

The MVP should demonstrate:

1. Both services run locally in Docker on NixOS.
2. No paid speech APIs or API keys are necessary.
3. Whisper uses GPU acceleration successfully.
4. Kokoro produces understandable Japanese speech using CPU inference.
5. The application can transcribe a spoken Japanese response.
6. The application can synthesize Japanese prompts and conversational replies.
7. Both services can remain loaded concurrently within the available system memory.
8. Short spoken interactions feel responsive.
9. Transcription failures and ambiguous results are handled without misleading feedback.
10. The speech services can be replaced independently of the curriculum engine.

Measure:

- STT transcription latency.
- STT accuracy on learner speech.
- TTS generation latency.
- Time to first playable audio.
- CPU utilization during TTS.
- GPU utilization during STT.
- Peak and idle system RAM.
- VRAM usage.
- Japanese speech naturalness and intelligibility.

Do not invent performance targets before obtaining a baseline.
